Annotated Bibliography

Sources used to research mobile computer vision and sign detection.

About These Sources

The sources below were used to learn about sign detection, computer vision, accessibility, object detection, and the challenges of detecting signs in real-world environments.

The sources are listed in the order in which they are introduced in the tutorial. Each annotation explains what the source contributed to the research and why it is useful for this topic.

[1] Real-Time Sign Detection for Accessible Indoor Navigation

Cheraghi, Seyed Ali, Giovanni Fusco, and James M. Coughlan. 2021.

Source: PMC / Sensors

Synopsis: This paper presents a computer vision system designed to detect signs for accessible indoor navigation. The research focuses on using a smartphone camera to detect signs and estimate information about their location. The system also considers camera orientation and other information that can be useful for navigation.

This source was especially useful for understanding how sign detection can be combined with mobile sensors and navigation instead of treating sign detection as a separate problem.

Reliability: High. This is a peer-reviewed research paper written by researchers working in computer vision and accessibility. It provides details about the proposed system and its evaluation.

[2] An Indoor Navigation App using Computer Vision and Sign Recognition

Fusco, Giovanni, Seyed Ali Cheraghi, and James M. Coughlan. 2020.

Source: PMC / Sensors

Synopsis: This paper describes iNavigate, an indoor navigation application that combines computer vision, smartphone sensors, and a floorplan to assist people who are blind or have low vision. The research demonstrates how signs can be detected and used as part of a larger navigation system.

The source helped explain why detecting a sign is only one part of an accessibility system. The system also needs to determine where the user is and provide useful directions.

Reliability: High. The paper is a scholarly research publication and describes the methods and system used by the researchers.

[3] TSingNet: Scale-aware and context-rich feature learning for traffic sign detection and recognition in the wild

Liu et al. 2021.

Source: ScienceDirect

Synopsis: This research focuses on traffic sign detection and recognition in real-world environments. The paper addresses difficult situations such as small signs and partially occluded signs. The proposed TSingNet approach uses scale-aware and context-rich feature learning to improve detection.

This source was useful for understanding why detecting small signs is difficult and how deep-learning models can use information at different scales and from the surrounding scene.

Reliability: High. This is a scholarly research paper published in an academic computer vision and image processing venue.

[4] Traffic-Sign Detection and Classification in the Wild

Zhu, Zhe, Dun Liang, Songhai Zhang, Xiaolei Huang, Baoli Li, and Shimin Hu. 2016.

Source: CVF Open Access

Synopsis: This paper investigates traffic-sign detection using the TT100K dataset, which contains traffic signs captured in real-world environments. The researchers developed a deep-learning-based detection system designed to locate signs at different sizes.

This source was useful because it shows the importance of large real-world datasets and multi-scale detection when signs are not all the same size or presented in controlled conditions.

Reliability: High. The paper was published at the IEEE/CVF Conference on Computer Vision and Pattern Recognition, a major computer vision research conference.

[5] D-patches: Effective Traffic Sign Detection with Occlusion Handling

Rehman, Yawar, Irfan Riaz, Xue Fan, and Hyunchul Shin. 2017.

Source: IET Research

Synopsis: This research focuses on detecting traffic signs when parts of the signs are blocked or hidden. The D-patches approach uses local visual information from different parts of a sign and combines the information to determine whether a sign is present.

This source was useful for understanding why occlusion is an important problem. A computer vision system cannot always depend on seeing an entire sign before making a detection.

Reliability: High. This is a scholarly research publication that describes a specific method for handling occluded traffic signs.

[6] Occlusion Reasoning for Object Detection under Arbitrary Viewpoint

Hsiao, Edward, and Martial Hebert. 2012.

Source: Carnegie Mellon University Robotics Institute

Synopsis: This research examines object detection when objects are viewed from different viewpoints and may be partially occluded. The researchers use 3D information and reasoning about visibility to improve object detection.

Although this paper is not specifically about signs, it is useful for understanding the more general computer vision problem of detecting objects when their appearance changes because of viewpoint and occlusion.

Reliability: High. The source comes from Carnegie Mellon University researchers and was presented at a major computer vision research conference.

[7] Detecting Signage and Doors for Blind Navigation and Wayfinding

Wang, Zun, Xiaodong Yang, and Yi Tian. 2013.

Source: PMC

Synopsis: This paper investigates computer vision methods for detecting signs and doors to help people who are blind navigate indoor environments. It discusses several traditional computer vision techniques, including edge detection, shape information, color and texture, and machine-learning methods.

The source was useful for comparing earlier traditional computer vision approaches with newer deep-learning-based object detection methods.

Reliability: High. This is a published research paper that describes the methods used for indoor signage and door detection.

[8] Researcher Interview

James M. Coughlan, personal email response to Vanessa Rodriguez, 2026.

I also contacted James M. Coughlan, one of the researchers involved in previous work on computer vision and accessible indoor navigation.

Coughlan explained that mobile sign recognition can be difficult when signs are far away, contain small text, or are viewed while the camera is moving. He also discussed motion blur, limited camera field of view, and the problem of having many signs and pieces of text in the environment.

He suggested that future systems could use panoramas to examine a wider area and could allow users to request a specific type of sign, such as a restroom or elevator sign.

His responses were useful because they connected the research discussed in this tutorial to practical problems that can occur when a person actually uses a mobile camera while navigating.

[9] NavPal: Smartphone-Based Navigation for Blind Travelers

Carnegie Mellon University, NavPal Project.

Source: Carnegie Mellon University - NavPal

Synopsis: NavPal is a Carnegie Mellon University research project focused on improving navigation for people who are blind or deafblind. The project developed smartphone-based tools for navigation in unfamiliar indoor environments and also explored route planning and accessible building maps.

This source was useful for understanding how computer vision and mobile technology can be part of a larger accessibility system. It also shows that an assistive navigation system needs to consider the user's needs, preferences, and the environment instead of only detecting objects.

Reliability: High. This is an official Carnegie Mellon University research project page describing work conducted by researchers in the university's Robotics Institute.

[10] NavCog: A Navigational Cognitive Assistant for the Blind

Ahmetovic, Dragan, Cole Gleason, Chengxiong Ruan, Kris M. Kitani, Hironobu Takagi, and Chieko Asakawa. 2016.

Source: Carnegie Mellon University

Synopsis: This research describes NavCog, a navigation system designed to assist people with visual impairments. The system uses a smartphone to provide turn-by-turn navigation and uses information from Bluetooth beacons and smartphone sensors to estimate the user's location.

This source was useful for understanding how mobile navigation systems can combine information from different sensors to provide useful assistance. It also shows how sign detection and other computer vision techniques could be combined with localization and navigation rather than being used by themselves.

Reliability: High. This is a research paper by Carnegie Mellon University and IBM Research researchers that was published as part of the MobileHCI 2016 conference.

[11] More Automation Isn't Always Better

Lindahl, Mallory. 2026.

Source: Carnegie Mellon University - Robotics Institute

Synopsis: This Carnegie Mellon University article describes a 2026 study examining how blind people interact with an assistive navigation robot in a real-world museum environment. The study found that participants changed between relying on the robot and navigating independently depending on the situation. The research highlights the importance of giving users control over how and when assistive technology provides help.

This source was useful for the future direction of this tutorial. It supports the idea that a future mobile sign detection system should not constantly report every sign it sees. Instead, the user could choose what type of information they need and when they want the system to provide it.

Reliability: High. This is an official Carnegie Mellon University Robotics Institute research news article describing a study accepted for CHI 2026 and identifying the researchers involved.
